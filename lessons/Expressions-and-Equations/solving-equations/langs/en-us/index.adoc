- This lesson re-frames "solving for @math{x}" as a _series of transformations_, in which an equation is re-structured over and over until the solution is found.
- There are many possible transformations that will lead to a solution! We recommend giving students the same problem, allowing each to solve it their own way, and then talk through their solutions so the whole can see different approaches. This is central to the kind of mathematical flexibility needed for equation-solving.

== Equivalent Equations
@objective{Identify which properties can be applied to transform a given equation}

=== Overview
Students extend their understanding of equivalent expressions to include equivalent _equations_.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

@vocab{Equivalence} is the property of two expressions meaning the same thing, even if they look different. And you've mastered a _lot_ of moves that preserve equivalence, allowing us to simplify and solve expressions:

- Commutativity
- Associativity
- Additive and Multiplicative Identity
- Additive and Multiplicative Inverse
- Distribution

*Can we use these same moves to help us solve _equations_?*

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

Each row in the table below holds a pair of equivalent equations. The first two are equivalent because of the @vocab{Commutative Property}: swapping the @math{13} and @math{x} does not change the meaning, so the second equation is @vocab{equivalent} to the first. What about the other equation pairs? What properties make them equivalent?

=== Investigate
@slidebreak{Investigate}

When solving an equation, *the goal is to get the unknown variable by itself* on one side of the equals sign. Each of these properties is a "move" that we can make to rewrite the equation in a way that gets us closer to putting the variable by itself. Let's get some practice with these moves!

@lesson-instruction{
- Complete @printable-exercise{spotting-moves.adoc}, and see if you can identify the moves that turn one equation into another (equivalent) one.
}

@teacher{If students are having trouble seeing the structure of any of these equations, have them sketch the Circle of Evaluation. Once students have completed these worksheets, review the solutions with students. If two students disagree about any of the answers, have the discussion!}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

You've had some practice spotting moves made by _other_ people. Can you make those moves yourself?

@lesson-instruction{
- Complete @printable-exercise{making-moves.adoc} to practice transforming equations yourself!
}

=== Synthesize
@QandA{
@Q{When looking at an equation, what do you pay attention to, when finding the best move?}
@A{The operation next to the variable tells us what to do: if something is being added, we use the additive inverse; if something is being divided, we use multiplicative inverse;}

@Q{Could there be more than one "best" move, where each choice is equally helpful?}
@A{When looking only at 1-step equations, most students will probably say no.}
@A{If a student says yes, stop and have them explain their thinking (ideally with an example equation).}
}

== Transforming and Solving Equations
@objective{Justify the use of one property over another when trying to solve an equation}

=== Overview
Students apply their algebraic properties to transform equations in different ways, and evaluate whether each transformation brought them closer to a solution.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}
By now, you know how to _spot_ a move, and you know how to _make_ a move. Let's use those skills to solve some simple equations!

Consider the following equation:

[.bigIndentedMath]
@math{x + 8 = 26}

@lesson-instruction{
Discuss with your partner or group:

- Can we use the Commutative Property here? The Associative Property? Distribution? Identity?
- Turn to @printable-exercise{what-moves-can-we-make.adoc}, and *complete the middle column* to explore how each of the properties might transform this equation.
}

@teacher{Review student answers for the middle column of each row.}

=== Investigate
@slidebreak{Investigate}

When solving an equation, knowing what moves we can make is the critical first step. But it's not enough just to know which moves we _can_ make -- we want to know which moves are the most useful in finding the answer!

@lesson-instruction{
- Return to @printable-exercise{what-moves-can-we-make.adoc}, and *complete the last column* to decide which moves are actually helpful.
- Which move do you think is the _best_ move to make here? Be ready to discuss!
}

@teacher{Solicit student responses, making sure to have them explain their thinking. Point out that @math{x + 0 = 18} can be transformed into @math{x = 18} thanks to the additive identity property!}


@slidebreak{Investigate}
Let's explore another equation:

[.bigIndentedMath]
@math{−15 + n = −9}

@lesson-instruction{
- Use @printable-exercise{1-step-equations.adoc} to identify all the moves you can make.
- If you're not sure, try converting each side of the equation to a Circle of Evaluation.
- Choose your move, and write the transformed equation.
- Can you solve? Is there another move to make?
- For more practice solving 1-step equations, tackle @opt-printable-exercise{1-step-equations-2.adoc}.
}

@strategy{Building Thinking Classrooms}{
Rather than use this workbook page, consider putting the three equations up on the wall! Have students work in groups of 2-4, repeating the equations if necessary so that students who worked on the same equation can share their notes. The instructions for students are the same:

1. What moves can you make here?
2. Which move is the _best?_
3. Rewrite the equation and solve.

Optional: have students first translate the equations into a Circle of Evaluation.
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}
All of those equations had one thing in common: _we needed to transform the equation to get rid of one number, in order to get the variable by itself._ Sometimes we needed to add or subtract, and sometimes we needed to multiply or divide.

But what if we needed to do both?

@lesson-instruction{
- Use @printable-exercise{2-step-equations.adoc} to identify all the moves you can make.
- If you're not sure, try converting each side of the equation to a Circle of Evaluation.
- Choose your move, and write the transformed equation.
- Can you solve? Is there another move to make?
- For more practice solving 2-step equations, tackle @opt-printable-exercise{2-step-equations-2.adoc}.
}

=== Synthesize
@slidebreak{Synthesize}

@QandA{
@Q{When looking at an equation like @math{3x + 5 = 20}, how do you know whether to use additive inverse or multiplicative inverse first?}
@A{The structure of the math on the left-hand side of the equals sign (in this case, @math{3x + 5}) shows that the multiplication happens "inside" of the addition, making addition the more likely place to start.}
@A{Drawing the Circle of Evaluation for the left-hand side of the equation makes it clear that additive inverse is probably the way to start.}

@Q{Could there be more than one "best" move, where each choice is equally helpful?}
@A{When looking only at 1-step equations, most students will probably say no.}
@A{If a student says yes, stop and have them explain their thinking (ideally with an example equation).}
