== Introduction to Statistical Language Modeling
@objective{ai-slm}

=== Overview
Students explore how text prediction relies on statistical language modeling.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

While texting, emailing, or even conducting a web search, you've probably noticed that your thoughts and sentences are sometimes finished for you! Predictive text is now a common feature of many apps, and it is made possible by a certain form of AI: the @vocab{language model}.

@slidebreak{Launch}

@lesson-instruction{
Consider this start of a phrase: "Close the..."

- Write down _at least three_ possible words that would come next
- Put a star next to the word that you think is the _most likely_
- Come up with another word that technically _could_ follow "close the...", but is very unusual
}

@teacher{
Call on students one-at-a-time to volunteer their answers. After each one, ask the class to raise their hands if it was one of the words they came up with, and make a tally chart of each word and the number of people who wrote it down. You don't need to tally every possible word - a top-10 list is great.
}

=== Investigate
@slidebreak{Investigate}

Let's look a little more closely at the chart we generated.

@QandA{
@Q{Which words were suggested most often?}
@Q{Which words were suggested least often?}
@Q{Are you surprised by the most and least common words? Why or why not?}
@Q{What fraction of our class proposed each of our top three words?}
@A{For example: If there are 25 students in your class, and 8 students chose “door”, it was chosen 8/25ths of the time.}
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

Statistical language models are built on the assumption that the fraction of the time that a word follows a series of words in a training corpus can help predict the likelihood of it being the right word to follow that same series of words in a future scenario. In this lesson, we will refer to that fraction as its @vocab{probability}.

@QandA{
@Q{Do you think a text messaging app with a predictive text feature would produce the same results as our class? Why or why not?}
@A{Answers will vary. Invite conversation.}
@A{An app will probably not suggest all the same words.}
@A{Our class is a pretty small sample, all from the same generation and geography and we are all currently at school so our minds are primed to respond differently than if we were somewhere else.}
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

Similar to other AI systems we've discussed, predictive text features are trained on a large amount of data, in this case, text.

During training, the computer builds a model:

1. It extracts each use of the phrase "Close the" from the corpus, along with the subsequent word.
2. It computes the probability of each possible next word following a given phrase.

When making a suggestion:

1. The computer queries the model for words that have a high probability of following "Close the" in the corpus.
2. It supplies that list to an algorithm that returns suggestions.

@slidebreak{InvestigateR}

@QandA{
@right{@image{images/texting-app.png, 200}} Consider this screenshot from a text messaging app.
@Q{Do you think the text messaging app would be good at generating text that sounds like someone in our class wrote it? Why or why not}
@A{Answers will vary based on the list your class generated.}
@A{This may be an opportunity for discussing the limitations / bias built into models from the training dataset.}

@Q{Would you expect your phone to suggest the same words as your classmates' phones would? Why or why not?}
@A{Invite discussion before advancing to the guided explanation.}
@A{This question is a great opportunity to revisit previous concepts, in particular the process of training and sampling that students discussed when thinking about @lesson-link{decision-trees-evaluating} and @lesson-link{regression-models-simple}.}
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

For a variety of reasons, not all phones will propose the same completions:

- The same phone won't necessarily propose the same word every time.
  * If there are multiple words with equal probability, some systems will select the word at random. 
  * Different messaging apps may be built using a different corpus. _(For example, Bootstrap authors notice big differences between predictions from imessage and WhatsApp.)_
- Not all phones and apps use the same corpus, the same prediction rules, or the same amount of randomness.
- A messaging app may also include a @vocab{model} of _the words that the owner uses_, to personalize suggestions to the way the user writes.
- Some predictive text features even account for the specific message being written! Typing the first letter of an unusual word that you already used in the same message could trigger the app to propose that unusual word.

@slidebreak{Investigate}

You have just considered the workings and in-context use of a @vocab{language model}. Hopefully you have discovered that, although it sometimes may _seem_ like your texting app can read your mind... it can't. It doesn't know the rules of grammar, the meanings of words, or your intentions when you are composing a text. Our model just knows @vocab{probability}, which it uses in ways that are often very impressive (but sometimes not!).

@teacher{
Throughout the lesson, we'll explore the very important "sometimes not" parenthetical, above.}

Using computers to process languages, often by consuming a corpus and constructing a model, is referred to as @vocab{Natural Language Processing}. In this lesson, we will explore in greater detail what exactly @vocab{Natural Language Processing} entails.

== Constructing a Statistical Language Model
@objective{ai-slm}
@objective{ai-slm-create}

=== Overview
Students construct a statistical language model by decomposing the text and computing the probabilities of different words following each other.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

The best way to make sense of statistical language modeling is to try it yourself! We'll start by constructing a model.

For our @vocab{training corpus}, we will start with an excerpt from the folk song _There was an Old Lady Who Swallowed a Fly_, which tells the nonsensical story of an old lady who swallows a fly, and the unfortunate series of events that follow.

@indented{_she swallowed the cat to catch the bird +
she swallowed the bird to catch the spider_
}

@lesson-instruction{
- Turn to @printable-exercise{counting-ngrams.adoc}.
- The first section on the page has this `short-corpus` on the left column.
- You'll also see a table of @vocab{n-grams} (strings of different lengths) that we can chop this corpus into.
}

@slidebreak{Launch}

To model text, computer scientists find all of the strings that can be generated from a corpus (one word at a time, two words at a time, etc.). They call these strings @vocab{n-grams} because they use @math{n} to represent the number of words in the chunk. For special cases where @math{n} is 1, 2, or 3, the @math{n}-grams are called @vocab{unigrams}, @vocab{bigrams}, and @vocab{trigrams}.

@QandA{
@Q{n-grams aren't the only things we describe with specialized words that begin with "uni", "bi", and "tri" (unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams). What other collections of words with these prefixes can you think of?}
@A{Answers will vary! Some possibilities might include:
  * unicycle, bicycle, tricycle
  * biceps, triceps, (quadriceps)
  * triangle, n-gon
  * (monolingual), bilingual, trilingual, quadrilingual
  * (monocle), binocular
  * (monomial), binomial, trinomial...
  * and lots of fun uses that aren't in a collection, like: unicorn, bikini, triceratops, trilogy, triplet, triathlon
  }
}

@slidebreak{Launch}

@lesson-instruction{

- Compare your unigram counts with another student, then complete the rows for the bigrams (size-2 n-grams).
- When you've compared your counts for the bigrams, finish the table by counting the trigrams (size-3 n-grams).

@Q{How was knowing the count of a particular @vocab{n-gram} in the @vocab{corpus} useful in determining the probability of the next word?}
@A{The count of any bigram, divided by the count of its first word gives us the probability of the first word being followed by the second word.}
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

@math{\frac
	{\textit{count(the bird)}}
	{\textit{count(the...)}} = \frac{2}{4} = 0.5 = 50}%

@slidebreak{Investigate}
The `stat-lang-model` function is where the _training_ happens: counting n-grams, computing probabilities, etc (and training a real LLM involves a *lot* more complexity beyond this!). @strategy{Large Language Models}{
Our language model is microscopic - a tiny corpus and a simple representation used for educational purposes. The language models most of us are familiar with, however, are Large Language Models. These use a corpus that is tens of billions of times larger, and employ a vastly more sophisticated representation: a neural network, which has been heavily optimized to minimize prediction error.
}

== Sampling from the Model
@objective{ai-slm-hallucination}
@objective{ai-slm-temperature}

=== Overview
Students use their statistical language model in a generative way, to produce output.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

Having built a language model, what can we do with it? We can use it in a generative way: we can produce output!

How might we go about doing that?

- We can start by choosing our first word. (One approach is to begin with the most common unigram in the corpus.)
- Next, we ask: "Given the first @vocab{unigram}, what is the most likely successor?"
- Then, we ask: "Given this @vocab{bigram}, what is the most likely successor?"
- We can continue asking: "Given this @vocab{n-gram}, what is the most likely successor?" for as long as we want.

@slidebreak{Investigate}
@lesson-point{Training is expensive. Inference is cheap, but adds up.}
When you talk to a chatbot, you're essentially asking something similar to `generate-text` to create responses to your prompts. And `generate-text` relies on `choose-suggestion` in our model, which is simply looking up `completions` based on the already-computed model.

Applying the `stat-lang-model` function to our corpus is where the @vocab{training} happens, resulting in a statistical language model. For real LLMs, this process can take _months_ and - if you've seen the news - use enormous amounts of power!

Asking a language model to come up with text (chatting, having it write a story, etc) is where @vocab{inference} happens. A single inference _doesn't use much computing power_, although it's important to remember just how many people might be using an LLM to make inferences every day.

=== Synthesize
@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{A model is a mathematical summary of a dataset}

@QandA{
@Q{Why does a statistical language model have it's name - what does it have to do with statistics?}
@A{It computes the probability of each word in the corpus appearing in the corpus and also the probability of each word following other words and phrases in the corpus.}

@Q{Explain why counting n-grams is needed to create the model?}
@A{If you don't count you can't find the probability.}
}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{All models are wrong. Some are useful.}

@QandA{
@Q{Why won't _any_ language model be *perfect*?}
@A{Because all it knows is a collection of words and phases found in the training corpus and various computed probabilities about those n-grams.}
@A{There is much more to language than probability!}

@Q{When is a language model *useful*?}
@A{When it's built using a corpus that is representative of the desired output.}
}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{Nous ne parlons pas toujours anglais!}
@QandA{
@Q{Might statistical language modeling be possible for other spoken human languages? Which languages?}
@A{Statistical language modeling will work for any language!}
@A{The AI does not need to "know" anything about the rules of grammar; it just follows rules that enable it to identify patterns.}
@A{A statistical language model built with a corpus of English text will not be very good at making suggestions in other languages!}

@Q{Can you think of other things besides human spoken languages that a similar approach might work for?}
@A{Answers will vary.}
@A{Statistical language modeling can process any "text" composed of symbolic notation as long as it uses spaces to separate the symbolic "words".}
@A{We will explore several examples in our lesson on @lesson-link{stat-lang-models-chatbots}, including sequences of game moves and musical notation.}
}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{Some people have raised concerns about ChatGPT and other language models.}
@QandA{
@Q{ChatGPT sometimes "makes stuff up." Why does this happen? What is actually going on?}
@A{When ChatGPT produces false or misleading information, it is not glitching nor is there a bug. ChatGPT is just doing what it does, following the model as it ought to.}

@Q{ChatGPT has biases that can be seen in its text output. Where do these biases come from?}
@A{If there are biases in the training corpus there will likely be biases in the output!}
@A{Real LLMs also use an extensive reinforcement learning phase, in which humans rate outputs of the model as positive or negative. The model is then adjusted to maximize positive responses. All humans possess some form of bias, which can leak into the model through this reinforcement.}
}
