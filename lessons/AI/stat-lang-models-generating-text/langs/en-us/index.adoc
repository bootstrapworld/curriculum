== Building a Statistical Language Model
@objective{ai-slm}
@objective{ai-slm-create}

=== Overview
Students construct a statistical language model by decomposing the text and computing the probabilities of different words following each other.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

While texting, emailing, or even conducting a web search, you've probably noticed that your thoughts and sentences are sometimes finished for you! Predictive text is now a common feature of many apps, and it is made possible by a certain form of AI: the @vocab{language model}.

@lesson-point{To model text, computer scientists find all of the strings that can be generated from a corpus (one word at a time, two words at a time, etc.).}

They call these strings @vocab{n-grams} because they use @math{n} to represent the number of words in the chunk. For special cases where @math{n} is 1, 2, or 3, the @math{n}-grams are called @vocab{unigrams}, @vocab{bigrams}, and @vocab{trigrams}.

== Sampling from the Model
@objective{ai-slm-hallucination}
@objective{ai-slm-temperature}

=== Overview
Students use their statistical language model in a generative way, to produce output.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{Launch}

Having built a language model, what can we do with it? We can use it in a generative way: we can produce output!

How might we go about doing that?

- We can start by choosing our first word. (One approach is to begin with the most common unigram in the corpus.)
- Next, we ask: "Given the first @vocab{unigram}, what is the most likely successor?"
- Then, we ask: "Given this @vocab{bigram}, what is the most likely successor?"
- We can continue asking: "Given this @vocab{n-gram}, what is the most likely successor?" for as long as we want.

@slidebreak{Launch}

Let's explore how Pyret can help us to generate text.

- Complete the first section with your partner using the @starter-file{ai-old-lady}.
}

@strategy{Vocabulary Note}{

*Next word -> token.* In our table, each row is a possible _next word_. Real language models do the same job, but they often don't work in whole words. They chop text into *tokens*: pieces that might be a whole word ("cat"), part of a word ("un" + "believ" + "able"), or even just punctuation or a space. A token is the unit the model predicts, just as a "next word" is the unit our model predicts. Our model treats every word as a single token, which keeps things simple, but the idea is identical: pick the most likely next token from a list of possibilities, each with a probability.

*Given -> context.* When we ask "_given_ this bigram, what comes next?", the bigram is the text the model looks at before predicting. In machine learning, that text is called the *context*. Our model's context is only the last _n_ words (the n-gram), while larger models use far more: whole paragraphs, or even entire conversations. The more context a model can see, the better its predictions tend to be.

Put together: _given_ a *context*, predict the next *token*. That is what our `completions` table does, and it is what every language model does.

If students ask how much context our model uses, the answer is the length of the n-gram we hand to `completions`: one word for a unigram, two for a bigram, and so on. Real models have a limit too, called the "context window", which is the maximum amount of text they can take into account at once.
}

@QandA{

@Q{How are Pyret's `completions`, `suggestions`, `choose-suggestion` and `generate-text` functions related?}
@A{`suggestions` builds an SLM with computed probabilities for are all of the `completions` plus all of the unigrams in the corpus.}
@A{`choose-suggestion` selects a single word from the `suggestions` table using the computed probabilities and a specified degree of randomness.}
@A{`generate-text` applies `choose-suggestion` over and over again.}

@Q{What is "heat" in the context of text generation?}
@A{The amount of randomness the gets applied to word selection with 0 being none and 100 being a lot.}

@Q{Why do you think it's important that we can choose what "heat" we want text to be generated with?}
@A{There are lots of different kinds of text! For example:
  * instructions for how to assemble a product should sound kind of robotic.
  * poetry shouldn't sound robotic at all.
  }

@Q{Should we always get the same output if we use the same input?}
@A{Answers will vary, but might include:
   * Sometimes there's more than one word with the same likelihood - at the very least we want to not always choose the same one of them.
   * Text would sound robotic if we always got the same output.
    *** The song doesn't always finish the sentence the same way.
    *** Humans wouldn't always finish the sentence exactly the same way - we came up with a variety of different words to finish "Close the..."
  * Functions can only have one output.
 }

@Q{What would the probability be for any word to follow "llama", which doesn't appear _at all_ in the original corpus?}
@A{The probability of following "llama" is the same for every word: undefined (@math{0 \div 0}).}

@Q{What should `generate-text` do if you ask it to suggest a word to follow "llama"?}
@A{Choose a word from the corpus.}
}

@slidebreak{Investigate}

Applying the `stat-lang-model` function to our corpus is where the @vocab{training} happens, resulting in a statistical language model. For real LLMs, this process can take _months_ and - if you've seen the news - _uses enormous amounts of power!_

Asking a language model to do something similar to our `generate-text` function (chat, write an essay, etc...) is where @vocab{inference} happens.  A single inference _doesn't use much computing power_.

@lesson-point{Training is expensive. Inference is cheap. But tens of millions of cheap inferences also add up to a sizable expense.}

=== Synthesize
@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{A model is a mathematical summary of a dataset.}

@QandA{
@Q{Why does a statistical language model have it's name - what does it have to do with statistics?}
@A{It computes the probability of each word in the corpus appearing in the corpus and also the probability of each word following other words and phrases in the corpus.}

@Q{Explain why counting n-grams is needed to create the model?}
@A{If you don't count you can't find the probability.}
}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{All models are wrong. Some are useful.}

@QandA{
@Q{Why won't _any_ language model be *perfect*?}
@A{Because all it knows is a collection of words and phases found in the training corpus and various computed probabilities about those n-grams.}
@A{There is much more to language than probability!}

@Q{When is a language model *useful*?}
@A{When it's built using a corpus that is representative of the desired output.}
}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{Nous ne parlons pas toujours anglais!}
@QandA{
@Q{Might statistical language modeling be possible for other spoken human languages? Which languages?}
@A{Statistical language modeling will work for any language!}
@A{The AI does not need to "know" anything about the rules of grammar; it just follows rules that enable it to identify patterns.}
@A{A statistical language model built with a corpus of English text will not be very good at making suggestions in other languages!}

@Q{Can you think of other things besides human spoken languages that a similar approach might work for?}
@A{Answers will vary.}
@A{Statistical language modeling can process any "text" composed of symbolic notation as long as it uses spaces to separate the symbolic "words".}
@A{We will explore several examples in our lesson on @lesson-link{stat-lang-models-chatbots}, including sequences of game moves and musical notation.}

@slidebreak{Synthesize}
@lesson-point{Machines do what they are programmed to do - no more, no less.}
@QandA{
@Q{Some people have raised concerns about ChatGPT and other language models because ChatGPT sometimes "makes stuff up." Why does this happen? What is actually going on?}
@A{When ChatGPT produces false or misleading information, it is not glitching, nor is there a bug. ChatGPT is just doing what it does, following the model as it ought to.}

@Q{ChatGPT has biases that can be seen in its text output. Where do these biases come from?}
@A{If there are biases in the training corpus there will likely be biases in the output!}
@A{Real LLMs also use an extensive reinforcement learning phase, in which humans rate outputs of the model as positive or negative. The model is then adjusted to maximize positive responses. All humans possess some form of bias, which can leak into the model through this reinforcement.}
}
