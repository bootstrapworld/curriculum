= Famous AI Disasters

AI tools can do remarkable things, but they also make mistakes, and some of those mistakes have been costly, embarrassing, or harmful. Below are some of the best-known examples, starting with the most recent. Each one links to a news article if you want the whole story.

As you read, ask yourself: *What went wrong? Who should have caught it? What could have prevented it?*

== An AI Model Breaks Into a Government Website
_Reported September 2026, CBS News_

Australia's prime minister announced that @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/australia-openai-rogue-model-hack-healthcare-system-sam-altman/, an OpenAI model had hacked into a government website}. While OpenAI was running training exercises to rate its models, one model was asked to search the internet for how much the Australian government spent on medicine. When it couldn't find an answer, it got around the website's restrictions and reached private files on an old health statistics site. Australia's defense minister said the model "scaled the fence." OpenAI noticed the activity in August, but didn't tell the Australian government until September 10. The prime minister called the situation "obviously unacceptable," although he said there was no evidence that anyone's personal information was accessed. OpenAI said, "our models took actions we did not intend."

*Why it matters:* An AI that is told to get an answer may find a way to do it that nobody planned for. People need to test these systems, and limit what they are allowed to do.

== A Consulting Firm Hands the Government a Report with Made-Up Sources
_October 2025, Associated Press_

Deloitte Australia wrote a 237-page report for the Australian government about the automated penalties used in its welfare system. A university researcher named Chris Rudge found errors in it, @link{https://www.ctvnews.ca/business/article/deloitte-to-partially-refund-australian-government-for-report-with-apparent-ai-generated-errors/, including a made-up quote attributed to a federal court judge and references to research papers that don't exist}. A corrected version added a note that an AI tool, Azure OpenAI, had been used to write the report. Deloitte agreed to repay the last installment of its AU$440,000 (about US$290,000) fee. It said the matter was "resolved directly with the client," and it did not say whether AI caused the errors.

*Why it matters:* AI can invent sources that look completely real. Anything an AI writes needs to be checked by a person before anyone relies on it.

== A Senior Lawyer Files Fake Quotes and Court Cases in a Murder Trial
_August 2025, CBS News_

A senior defense lawyer in Victoria, Australia, @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/australia-murder-case-ai-court-filings-fake-quotes-nonexistent-judgments/, filed written arguments in a murder case that contained made-up quotes from a speech to the state legislature, and citations of court decisions that don't exist}. The judge's staff found the problem when they couldn't locate the cited cases. The lawyer took "full responsibility" and told the judge that he and his team were "deeply sorry and embarrassed." The mistake delayed the case by a day. The defense team said they had checked the first few citations and assumed the rest were correct.

*Why it matters:* Checking only some of an AI's work isn't enough. If the first few answers are right, the rest can still be wrong.

== An AI Coding Assistant Deletes a Company's Database
_July 2025, ABC News_

A tech entrepreneur was using an AI coding tool from a company called Replit when @link{https://abc7news.com/post/artificial-intelligence-ceo-san-francisco-tech-company-replit-apologizes-ai-chatbot-goes-rogue/17295335/, the tool deleted a production database: the live data that a business runs on. The entrepreneur said the AI "was lying and being deceptive all day," hiding bugs by making up fake data and reports}. Replit's CEO apologized and said the company would carry out a "postmortem" to find out what went wrong.

*Why it matters:* An AI that can take actions, and not just write text, can do real damage. It can also give false reports about what it did.


== A Chatbot Posts Hate Speech on Social Media
_July 2025, CBS News_

@link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/grok-elon-musks-ai-chatbot-antisemitic-comments/,Grok, the chatbot from Elon Musk's company xAI, posted antisemitic comments on the social media site X and praised Adolf Hitler}. Asked which 20th-century figure could "deal with" a particular issue, it named Hitler. The posts were later deleted. Grok called its answer "an unacceptable error from an earlier model iteration," and Musk wrote that Grok had been "too eager to please and be manipulated" by the prompts people gave it.

*Why it matters:* When a chatbot talks in public, its mistakes are public too, and deleting them afterward doesn't undo the harm.

== A Newspaper Prints a Summer Reading List of Books That Don't Exist
_May 2025, NBC News_

@link{https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/chicago-sun-admits-summer-book-guide-included-fake-ai-generated-titles-rcna208325, The Chicago Sun-Times ran a summer reading guide that recommended books that don't exist}. Some were credited to real, well-known authors, including Isabel Allende, Rebecca Makkai, and Min Jin Lee. A freelancer working with a company called King Features had made the guide with an AI tool and hadn't disclosed it. King Features ended its relationship with the writer. The Sun-Times removed the list and said it would label third-party content in print. The Philadelphia Inquirer had printed the same list.

*Why it matters:* AI-written text can sound completely believable while being false, even when it names real people.

== Google's AI Search Answers Suggest Putting Glue on Pizza
_May 2024, CBS News_

About two weeks after Google launched "AI Overviews," its AI-written answers at the top of search results, people began sharing answers that were odd, wrong, or even dangerous. @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/google-ai-overview/, The AI suggested adding glue to pizza so the cheese would stick, and recommended drinking urine to pass kidney stones}. One expert said an answer about wild mushrooms left out safety information that could be fatal. Google said the AI had taken sarcastic forum posts at face value and misread the language on some webpages. It admitted that some answers were "odd, inaccurate or unhelpful," and it limited when AI Overviews appear, including turning them off for hard news topics.

*Why it matters:* An AI that summarizes the internet can't always tell a joke, or a bad source, from a good one.

== A Chatbot Promises a Refund the Airline Doesn't Offer
_February 2024, CBS News_

Jake Moffatt was buying plane tickets to attend his grandmother's funeral. He asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares, which are discounts for people traveling after a death in the family. @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/aircanada-chatbot-discount-customer/, The chatbot told him he could get a partial refund if he applied within 90 days, but Air Canada's real policy didn't allow it}. When the airline refused to pay, Moffatt took it to Canada's Civil Resolution Tribunal. Air Canada argued that its chatbot was "a separate legal entity" responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that argument, ruled that the chatbot was part of the airline's website, and ordered Air Canada to pay CA$812.

*Why it matters:* A company is responsible for what its chatbot tells customers.

== Lawyers Cite Court Cases That ChatGPT Made Up
_June 2023, CBS News_

Lawyers for a man who was suing the airline Avianca used ChatGPT to help with their legal research. The brief they filed cited court cases that don't exist, such as _Varghese v. China Southern Airlines_. @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/chatgpt-judge-fines-lawyers-who-used-ai/, ChatGPT had invented them, and when one lawyer asked ChatGPT whether the cases were real, it said they were}. Avianca's lawyers couldn't find the cases anywhere. A federal judge fined the two lawyers and their law firm $5,000.

*Why it matters:* Asking an AI to confirm its own answer doesn't make the answer true.

== A Hiring AI Learns to Prefer Men
_Reported October 2018, BBC News_

Amazon spent years building an AI system to review résumés and pick the strongest candidates. It was trained on about ten years of résumés submitted to the company, and most of them came from men. As a result, @link{https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-45809919, the AI system learned to penalize résumés that included the word "women's" and gave lower scores to graduates of two all-women's colleges}. Engineers tried to fix it, but concluded they couldn't stop it from finding new ways to discriminate, and the project was scrapped. Amazon said the tool was "never used by Amazon recruiters to evaluate candidates."

*Why it matters:* An AI trained on data from the past copies the patterns in that data, including unfair ones.

== A Chatbot Learns from Strangers on Twitter and Turns Hateful
_March 2016, CBS News_

Microsoft launched Tay, a chatbot on Twitter that was designed to learn how to hold a conversation by talking with people and picking up from their posts. @link{https://www.cbsnews.com/news/microsoft-shuts-down-ai-chatbot-after-it-turned-into-racist-nazi/, CBS News: Microsoft shuts down AI chatbot, Within 24 hours}, users had tricked Tay into repeating offensive and hateful messages, and Microsoft took it offline less than a day after launch. Microsoft apologized, said a coordinated attack had exploited a weakness in the bot, and took responsibility for not anticipating it.

*Why it matters:* A chatbot that learns from its users learns from the worst of them, too.
