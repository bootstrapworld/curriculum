== Case Studies

=== Overview
Students break into groups and read one of three case studies, each dealing with a different issue in Data Science. They discuss the implications of each, then share back.

=== Launch
@slidebreak{LaunchR}

@right{@image{images/AtomicBomb.png, 150}}

During World War II, scientists were engaged in a race to develop new weapons, more powerful than anything the world had ever seen. While the immediate goal was "win the war", many of the scientists realized that the weapons they were developing could be used for all sorts of things _after the war was over_ -- and not all of them were good.

History is filled with examples of those who study science -- be it medicine, human behavior, psychology, etc. -- getting caught up in research and forgetting about the humanity and well-being of the people they were studying. Over time, governments and scientific communities have developed @link{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidelines_for_human_subject_research, guidelines for ethical human experimentation}. 

@scrub{
  * Between 1932 and 1972, doctors in the U.S. studied nearly 400 African American men, as part of @link{https://en.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuskegee_Syphilis_Study, an experiment about the long-term effects of syphillis going untreated}, without informing the men that treatment was widely available and they would never be treated. 
  * In 1971, researchers at Stanford conducted @link{https://en.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_prison_experiment, a prison simulation experiment} to study human behavior. Some of the guards' behavior allegedly resulted in dangerous and psychologically damaging situations. Despite the fact that participants were told they had the right to leave at any time, the researchers did not allow this.
}

@slidebreak{Launch}

*Should data scientists be bound by any ethical rules? If so, what rules?*

Tech companies are collecting massive datasets on users for data scientists to mine. In order to make recommendations, they build profiles on everyone, figuring out what their preferences are and where they tend to spend their time. While knowing our age or whether we're more likely to go to a movie, play or concert helps them to get us the information we need, these datasets and profiles could be used for far more than that. 

What if the FBI used them to try and figure out who is likely to commit a crime?
Or a company tries to learn prospective employees' religion, sexual orientation or voter registration?

As Data Scientists build ever-more sophisticated models based on ever-more accurate datasets, they need to think about the ethics of what they're doing as well!

=== Synthesize
@slidebreak{Synthesize}
Give students time to discuss and share back. Encourage students to share back differing views on the articles.

@QandA{
@Q{What are some commonalities among the issues raised by these articles?}
@A{Data collected about us and others like us is being used to make predictions about our future behavior, whether that be what ads we'll be susceptible to or how likely we are to commit crimes.}
@A{People are not fully aware of how data is being used to construct profiles about them.}
@A{People are not always comfortable with how much companies know about them from their shopping history, etc.}

@Q{What are some differences among the issues raised by these articles?}
@A{No harm is done if target sends us an ad we aren't interested in.}
@A{Deciding how to sentence someone based on racially-biased algorithms locks low risk people up and frees high risk people to commit more crimes.}
}

== Additional Exercises
@slidebreak{Supplemental}

- Generate a list of "Rules for Ethical Data Science"?
- Write a one-page essay about your thoughts on the future of AI.
- Write arguments for or against using algorithms to make decisions. 
- Host a debate, giving students a chance to make "opening" and "closing" arguments, taking turns so that the closer for each side can respond to what the other side said.
- Have students write a response to an article or clip.
